Superuser detection · selective cheating

The cheat who sees only a few players

He reads the cards of one or two opponents and plays honestly against everyone else. His overall numbers look ordinary; his results against those few opponents do not.

The idea

A cheat may see the cards of only a few opponents. Against those few he plays like a superuser. Against everyone else he has to guess, like any other player.

Why his overall numbers look ordinary

Picture a few thousand hands against his victims inside many thousands of honest ones. His win rate, his showdowns and his folds are averages over all of them, so the edge melts into the honest play. On the simulator, a planted cheat who plays a fifth of his heads-up hands against his one victim ranks 16th on win rate in a field of 300 players, and between 19th and 180th on the showdown and pot-size numbers: a good regular, not an obvious cheat.

Answer one: rank every pair

rows: player A · columns: opponent B · one square = the pair “A against B”

Score every pair “A against B” in the field: how A does against that one opponent. Then rank all the pairs against each other. A cheat with two victims can put two pairs at the top, however ordinary his overall numbers are.

Pairs are for selective cheating. A cheat who sees the whole table beats everybody, so his pairs usually do not stand out: on the simulator the best pair of such a cheat ranked between 9th and 385th (one who started late reached 3rd). The per-player tests catch that kind.

Fair to the weak player

A raw win rate is not enough: a weak player who loses to everybody would light up every pair against him. So each pair is measured beyond two things: what A does to everyone else, and what everyone else does to B. What is left is what A does to B alone. The overall average is added back once, so it is not taken away twice.

  • win rate
  • showdowns won
  • calls that were ahead
  • bets that were ahead
  • equity when the money went in
  • preflop calls and raises that were ahead
  • folds that were behind

Each part is measured the same way and needs a minimum sample. The score adds the parts’ z-scores and divides by the square root of the sum of their correlations: parts that read the same hands (the win rate and the showdowns above all) are not counted twice, and an honest pair scores around 0 with a spread of about 1. A pair is ranked once it has two parts. The fold part (shaded) needs every card, folded hands included.

Answer two: look at windows

He may cheat only for a while. Over his whole history the edge is diluted again; inside a window of a few thousand hands it is not. Windows are a review practice, not a separate automatic test: load the hands of a period into a dataset of their own and run the same tools on it. The screen already keeps every player’s last 30 and last 15 days and a weekly series with a change point, but its pair table covers the whole dataset.

What the report shows

A sketch. The labels use the 2,532 ranked pairs of one simulated field.

The per-suspect report lists the opponents he beats beyond his own level: the victims table, with each part, the score and the pair’s place among all ranked pairs. On the suspicious-hands page the label “pair #k of N (top x%)” sits next to a victim’s id, and only a victim’s. Other opponents get no label, so the hands against a victim stand out. The victims table also sits at the top of that page.

What a victim label means

A pair gets the label when its score is at least 3 and it ranks in the top 1% of all ranked pairs (or the top 10, whichever is larger). The score adds up parts that read the same hands, so it is scaled for how much they move together; an honest pair then scores around 0 with a spread of about 1, and 3 is about three of those spreads out. The label is a reason to look, not a verdict. On the simulator, in a field of 300 players, the three planted pairs are the top three of 2,532 ranked pairs and none of the 293 honest players gets the label; in a six-max field of 200 players the planted pair is first of 2,340 and 3 of the 194 honest players get one. In those two fields the planted pairs score 7.0 to 9.2 and the best honest pair 2.9 to 3.8.

What to do

  1. Start from the pairs.Open the field’s top pairs, or the suspect’s victims table, before his overall numbers.
  2. Read the hands against the victim first.The label marks them on the suspicious-hands page. The Equity review box under a hand says whether the betting, or only the opponent’s real cards, explains his decision.
  3. See which parts carry the score.Several parts pointing the same way (money, showdowns, calls, bets, folds) make a stronger lead than one part on a small sample.
  4. Look at a window.Load the days on which the two met most into a dataset of their own and run the same tools on it; do the same for a period before them.
  5. Treat the label as a lead.Honest pairs reach the top 1% too. A person reads the hands and decides.
